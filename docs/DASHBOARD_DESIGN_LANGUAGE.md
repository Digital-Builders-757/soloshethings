# Solo SHE Things — Dashboard Design Language

The dashboard is not a control panel. It is not a utility grid, a SaaS workspace, or a component collection. It is an editorial member environment — a place that a person inhabits, not a tool they operate.

The design intention is grounded in warmth, compositional restraint, material depth, and editorial pacing. Atmosphere precedes interaction. The goal is that a person arriving at the dashboard feels something before they read anything — a quality of light, a sense of weight, a suggestion of warmth — and that this sensation persists as the underlying emotional register of every action taken within it.

Every decision documented here was made to serve that intention. Nothing in this system exists for visual interest alone.

---

## Section 1 — Philosophy

### 1.1 Atmosphere over Interface

The dashboard should feel like a place before it reads as software.

This distinction is not rhetorical. In most interface design, the visual language announces its own presence: borders declare edges, shadows indicate depth, animations signal responsiveness. The user perceives the interface as a system they are operating. Every interaction confirms: *this is software*.

The alternative is an environment. An environment does not announce itself. Light exists in it without you asking why. Temperature exists without you noticing a heater. The space feels inhabited because its conditions are simply present. When you interact with an object inside it, the object responds — but the environment itself does not change its character.

The dashboard achieves this through layered atmospheric conditions: ambient radial warmth fields at near-invisible opacity, subconscious tonal variation across page regions, paper-stock surface modulation, and environmental depth planes. None of these layers are individually perceptible. Their combined effect is that the page does not feel flat.

The practical test: a user should feel the atmosphere before they consciously notice design. If they say "this has a nice texture" or "I like the gradient there," the atmosphere has failed — it has become decoration. The correct response is something closer to "this feels warm" or "this feels considered," without the ability to point at why.

Specific implementation principles:
- Ambient radial fields are placed where light would naturally pool if this were a physical surface: upper-left quadrant of hero panels, lower-left of utility sections, slightly off-center.
- Background opacity on containers is low enough that the page warmth reads through them. Containers suggest surfaces rather than declare themselves.
- Environmental depth is achieved through tonal hierarchy, not visible shadow stacking.

---

### 1.2 Editorial Composition

Editorial design — in its print tradition — is not about making content attractive. It is about directing attention through space and time. A reader of a magazine does not experience a page as a grid. They experience it as a series of moments with pacing: something pulls them in, something holds them, something releases them to the next thing.

The dashboard inherits this principle directly.

Composition is asymmetric by design. Tiles are not perfectly aligned. Modules are staggered. The Identity Record sidebar enters the reading flow later than the main column — it is not a panel you notice first; it is where your attention arrives after traveling through the primary composition. The featured tile drifts 8px off its column boundary. Secondary tiles carry micro-offsets of 4 to 12 pixels. These are not errors. They are authored marks.

The principle: **nothing should feel auto-generated, even when generated by systems.**

A grid produces equality. Equality produces mechanical rhythm. Mechanical rhythm produces the sensation that no human made a decision about the layout — that the system assembled itself. That sensation, however subtle, removes the impression of authorship. Authorship is what makes a premium space feel curated rather than constructed.

Optical balance is preferred over mathematical balance. Two elements can be visually equal in weight without sharing identical dimensions. A smaller, darker object has equivalent visual mass to a larger, lighter one. Decisions about placement, spacing, and scale respect optical weight, not pixel measurement.

The reading flow is intended as:
1. Hero headline — emotional entry point
2. Featured utility tile — compositional anchor
3. Supporting tile cluster — navigational landscape
4. Identity Record — quiet resting point

Each stage has different visual density, spacing cadence, and typographic weight. The eye is guided without being herded.

---

### 1.3 Materiality

Digital surfaces are frictionless and perfectly flat. They have no weight, no temperature, no grain. This frictionlessness is efficient. It is not warm.

The dashboard introduces material qualities without making them visible.

Paper rarely warms evenly. Under diffuse light, a page is cooler at its edges and slightly warmer toward its center — where the reader's attention falls, where ambient light accumulates. Tonal modulation layers inside key containers replicate this: a near-invisible gradient from cool-white at the top edge to faint amber warmth at the bottom produces the sensation of paper responding to environment without any visible colour treatment.

Ink has a different relationship with surface than digital type. Printed type has slight absorption — it sinks into paper rather than sitting on it. The heading colour palette (`#713522` rather than a sharper dark red-brown) approximates ink absorbed into warm stock. The difference between these two colours is approximately 7 units on a perceptual colour scale — not visible in direct comparison but registered subconsciously as softer or harder.

Surface breakup: a perfectly smooth dark surface reads as digital. A surface with micro-dot texture at 0.035 opacity using `mix-blend-soft-light` reads as printed. The texture is below conscious visibility on standard displays — its function is to deny perfection, not to add visible pattern.

The governing rule for all material effects: **effects should exist below the visibility threshold.** If a viewer can identify what they are looking at — "there is a texture here" or "there is a gradient here" — the effect is too strong. The correct calibration is that the viewer feels the result without being able to name the cause.

---

### 1.4 Restraint

Premium interfaces characteristically remove more than they add. This is not minimalism as aesthetic preference. It is the recognition that every added element competes for attention, and attention is the scarcest resource in the experience.

Restraint in this system means:
- No decorative element exists without atmospheric justification.
- Motion is used to support emotional pacing, not to demonstrate capability.
- Shadows exist as warmth signals, not as depth declarations.
- Contrast is modulated to be warm rather than sharp.
- Visual hierarchy is established through weight and spacing, not through borders, pills, or accent bars.

The emotional target is sophistication, not spectacle. Spectacle produces initial engagement followed by fatigue. Sophistication produces long-term comfort. A space that feels expensive on first visit should feel equally expensive on the hundredth visit — which means it must achieve its quality through intrinsic character, not through effects that lose novelty.

This is why the dashboard does not use glassmorphism, heavy blur, neon accents, animated gradients, or aggressive hover feedback. These are techniques that announce the effort involved in creating them. Restraint conceals effort so that only the result is perceived.

When in doubt: subtract. A page with too much atmosphere is worse than a page with too little. Too little feels unfinished. Too much feels designed — and the distinction between "feels designed" and "feels considered" is precisely what this system is trying to maintain.

## Section 2 — Typography System

### 2.1 Display Typography

Fraunces is the only display typeface in this system. It is used for headlines, emotional moments, and the dominant compositional anchors of each section. It is not used for body text, metadata, or utility labels.

Fraunces carries several qualities that make it appropriate here. Its optical-size features produce slightly more ink at display sizes — thicker strokes that absorb warmth well and read as physically present on the page. Its italic variant has a distinct calligraphic energy that creates contrast against roman weights. This contrast — italic greeting against roman name, in the hero — establishes an emotional rhythm before a word has been consciously read.

Leading is set tighter than browser defaults at display sizes. At `1.05–1.1` for large headings, the type stack reads as a typeset block rather than a list of lines. This is optically correct for large weights: looser leading at display sizes reads as digital default, not typographic decision.

Letter-spacing is negative at large sizes (`-0.03em` to `-0.04em`). This counteracts the optical spread that occurs when type is rendered at large scale on screen, restoring the impression of type designed for print.

Weight hierarchy uses black weight for identity moments and dominant headings, italic for emotional context (greetings, secondary voice), and regular weight for supporting copy. The hierarchy is not about size alone — it is about the emotional register each weight carries.

---

### 2.2 Eyebrow System

Eyebrows are uppercase micro-labels set in small caps scale (`0.62rem`) with widely tracked letter-spacing (`0.22em–0.32em`). They appear above section headings, above module titles, and inside utility tiles.

Their function is compositional, not informational. An eyebrow does not tell the reader something they do not know. It establishes pacing — a breath before the heading, a signal that something is about to be said. Read aloud, a page with eyebrows has a different rhythm than one without. That difference is what eyebrows contribute.

Tracking increases with contextual importance. A section-level eyebrow (`0.32em`) has more authority than a tile-level eyebrow (`0.22em`). The wider spacing reads as slower, more deliberate — as if the text is taking more time before what follows.

Eyebrows do not use bold weight in this system. Uppercase + wide tracking at low opacity is already visually strong. Adding weight would push them from compositional whisper to UI label. They should function as a compositional element that the reader barely notices reading, but would notice the absence of.

---

### 2.3 Body and Metadata Typography

Body text and metadata use warm brown tones across a narrow palette: `#6d5849`, `#7a5e4a`, `#9a7258`. These are not grey neutrals. They are brown ink tones — desaturated enough to recede, warm enough to remain in the same temperature register as the page.

Pure black text is not used in this system. Black type on warm paper reads as imposed contrast — an outside element placed on a surface it does not belong to. Brown ink on warm stock reads as absorbed, native. The perceptual difference is the difference between a printed page and a printed sticker.

Line height for body text is `1.7` — generous but not loose. Generous leading reads as edited. Tight leading reads as compressed. `1.7` creates the impression that each line has room to breathe, that the copy was typeset by someone who cared about the reading experience.

Leading for small-scale labels and metadata is tighter: `leading-relaxed` (`1.625`) or `leading-snug` (`1.375`). Metadata is functional text — it should be readable, not editorial. The contrast in leading between body copy and metadata is itself a compositional signal.

## Section 3 — Composition System

### 3.1 Spatial Rhythm

The dashboard is divided into compositional zones with distinct spatial rhythms:

**Hero zone**: expansive. Large type, generous padding, atmospheric layering. The eye enters and slows. The hero is an arrival, not a menu.

**Editorial modules** (below hero): asymmetric. Three modules with unequal widths (`2.2fr 1.4fr 1.6fr`), unequal heights, and vertical offsets. The left module is grounded and dense. The center is directional and compact. The right is airy — the composition's resting point before the horizontal rule.

**Horizontal compositional anchor**: a single thin warm rule with generous vertical whitespace (`my-12–my-16`). This is the pause between the editorial modules and the utility system. It re-establishes pacing before the eye descends.

**Utility landscape**: staggered, asymmetric, variable density. The featured tile is wider and forward. Supporting tiles carry micro-offsets. The Identity Record sidebar enters the reading flow below the section heading with a deliberate vertical delay, creating a diagonal reading path rather than two parallel columns.

The rhythm is narrative: enter, pause, travel, rest.

---

### 3.2 Controlled Asymmetry

Asymmetry in this system is not arbitrary. It operates within strict constraints (4–12px maximum for tile offsets; 8px for the featured tile's horizontal drift) and serves a single purpose: removing the mechanical equality that makes layouts feel system-generated.

The principle: **asymmetry should be felt, not noticed.**

If a viewer consciously observes that a tile is offset, the offset is too large. At 4–12px, the offset is below the threshold of conscious measurement but above the threshold of perceptual pattern-breaking. The viewer registers the composition as organic without being able to identify why.

This applies at every scale: column widths (`1.02fr` vs `0.98fr`), gap ratios (column gap differs from row gap by `0.15rem`), sidebar width (`20.5rem` rather than a round number). None of these differences are individually identifiable. Together they produce the sensation that decisions were made by someone rather than delegated to a grid system.

---

### 3.3 Sidebar Delay Principle

The Identity Record sidebar is positioned with `lg:mt-12` — entering the reading flow 48px below the Quick Actions heading. This is intentional and precise.

A sidebar that aligns with the top of its adjacent content creates two parallel columns of equal compositional authority. The eye divides its attention between them, moving horizontally rather than following the primary reading path. The main content does not dominate.

By delaying the sidebar's entrance, the eye is directed first through the heading, then down through the tile cluster, and arrives at the sidebar only after the primary content has been encountered. The sidebar functions as a destination, not a competing starting point.

This is supplemented by the sidebar's reduced visual weight: lighter background opacity (`65%`), a top-border-only treatment (no perimeter box), and narrow width (`20.5rem`). The sidebar reads as a supporting column — a publication's contributor profile in the margin — not as a dashboard panel of equal importance to the main content.

---

### 3.4 Depth Planes

The composition operates on three perceptual planes:

**Foreground** — the featured utility tile. Established through: warmest tile background (sun tone), highest tonal density, base shadow at 5% opacity, horizontal drift off the column grid. These combine to create the perception that this tile sits slightly closer than the others.

**Midground** — standard utility tiles. Medium tonal weight. No base shadow. Variable heights and offsets create organic rhythm within this plane.

**Background** — the Identity Record. Established through: lightest background opacity, top-border-only treatment, diagonal entrance offset, wider gap between it and the utility field. The sidebar reads as embedded in the page rather than placed on it.

Depth hierarchy is achieved through tonal density, not through visible elevation. There are no stacked shadows, no z-index tricks, no layered borders. The planes exist as qualities of warmth and weight.

---

## Section 4 — Atmospheric System

### 4.1 Ambient Fields

Ambient fields are large blurred gradients — `h-48 to h-[28rem]`, `blur-3xl` — at very low opacity (4–18%). They are absolutely positioned behind all content.

Their function is environmental, not decorative. The distinction: a decorative gradient is placed to make a region look attractive. An environmental field is placed to make a region feel warm, or cool, or deep — as light would in a physical space.

Placement is intentional. Upper-left fields warm the reading entry point. Upper-right fields create diagonal atmospheric tension. Mid-page brown fields add tonal gravity as the eye descends. Lower-section fields (introduced at `top-[58rem]`) fill the atmospheric gap beneath the editorial modules — without them, the utility section sits on a perceptually flat plane.

Multiple overlapping fields at very low opacity produce the sensation of ambient warmth varying unevenly across the surface — which is how any real warm environment behaves. The viewer does not perceive individual sources. They perceive a space that is simply warmer in some areas than others.

---

### 4.2 Warmth Pooling

Warmth pools are localised radial gradients inside specific containers. They differ from ambient fields in scale and purpose: they introduce the specific physical quality of light settling into one region of a surface.

Real paper under natural light is not uniformly illuminated. Light arrives from an angle, settles in corners and along edges, creates subtle hot spots in the regions that face the source. A warmth pool at `circle at 24% 32%` — upper-left quadrant — places the warmth where it would be if the tile were a piece of paper held toward a window.

All warmth pools are set at `opacity-[0.045]` or lower. At this level, they contribute to the tactile sensation of the surface without producing a visible gradient. The test: cover the tile with a solid colour and remove the pool, then uncover — if the pool's presence was noticeable when covered, it is too strong.

---

### 4.3 Contour Backgrounds

Contour backgrounds are SVG systems of concentric ellipses that create topographic depth behind module content. They evoke cartographic contour maps, architectural ground-plan drawings, and the tonal ring patterns found in wood grain and paper fibres.

They should never read as illustrations. At opacity `0.36–0.62`, with stroke widths of `0.11–0.13`, they exist at the edge of conscious perception. A viewer might notice a faint pattern behind the text if they look specifically for it. Looking at the text, they see nothing unusual — but the surface does not read as flat.

Their function is to give large text-heavy containers a sense of depth and material complexity without adding visible decoration. They are most effective in modules where the content is dense and the container needs to feel textured.

---

### 4.4 Grain and Texture Philosophy

Grain overlays use SVG `feTurbulence` filters to produce paper-like noise patterns. Their purpose is to reintroduce physical variation — the micro-irregularity that all material surfaces carry and that screens uniformly lack.

The governing rule: **if the texture becomes consciously visible, it is too strong.**

Grain applied at `intensity: medium` inside the orange hero panel is at the edge of this threshold on most displays. At retina resolutions it becomes invisible. At standard 96dpi it is barely present. This calibration is intentional: the texture exists as a perception modifier, not as a visible element.

Grain is applied only where it serves a specific material purpose — inside the dark art panel where digital smoothness would read as artificial, and on the Identity Record where paper-stock tactility reinforces the "contributor profile" reading.

It is not applied globally. Grain everywhere becomes wallpaper. Used selectively, it reads as material quality.

## Section 5 — Interaction Philosophy

### 5.1 Hover Behaviour

Hover in this system is atmospheric, not reactive.

A reactive hover announces the presence of an interactive element. The object changes size, brightness, or position in response to the cursor's arrival — the message is: *you are hovering over me.* This interaction model is correct for productivity software. It creates clear affordances.

An atmospheric hover does not announce itself. The object shifts very slightly — 1px vertically — and a faint warm shadow arrives around it. The shadow is at 5.9% and 3.3% opacity for its two layers. At these values, the shadow is below the threshold of conscious attribution. The viewer does not think "a shadow appeared." They feel a slight warmth shift, a barely perceptible sense that something changed.

The question the viewer should have after hovering: *did something actually happen?*

That ambiguity is the target. It positions the interface as a space that breathes rather than a system that responds. The emotional register shifts from tool-use to environment-inhabiting.

The `dash-card-lift` transition uses `cubic-bezier(0.22, 1, 0.36, 1)` at `300ms` for transform and `340ms` for shadow. The deceleration is slow enough to feel deliberate, not instant. The total travel (1px vertical, barely-there shadow) is small enough to question.

---

### 5.2 Motion System

All entrance animations use the CINEMATIC cubic-bezier: `[0.22, 1, 0.36, 1]`.

This curve begins with slow acceleration and decelerates to a complete stop — the inverse of a spring. Springs return energy; this curve absorbs it. The result is a motion that *arrives* rather than *snaps*. Elements appear as if settling into position, not as if triggered by an event.

Entrance animations use `opacity: 0 → 1` combined with `y: 24px → 0`. The 24px vertical travel means elements arrive from slightly below — they rise into position rather than appearing in place. This creates the sensation of gravity: the composition has weight.

Stagger intervals are `0.14s` between children, with an initial `delayChildren` of `0.08s`. These intervals are calibrated to create a reading sequence — the eye follows the motion down the page, tracking the order in which things arrive. This is not a loading animation. It is a compositional entrance.

Duration at `0.65s` is the minimum for the deceleration to feel considered rather than hurried. Below `0.5s` the CINEMATIC curve cannot complete its deceleration within perception — the motion reads as fast, not slow and deliberate.

---

### 5.3 Animation Restraint

Motion exists in this system for one purpose: to support the emotional tone of the environment.

Motion does not exist to demonstrate technical capability. It does not exist to signal that the interface is "alive." It does not exist to entertain.

This means: no looping animations on the primary interface plane. No bouncing. No aggressive scaling. The `AmbientField` component drifts at `24–30s` cycle times and `0.45` opacity — if it were shorter or stronger, it would become a visible animation rather than an ambient condition. At 24 seconds, a full cycle takes half a minute. A viewer would need to watch with intent to perceive the movement. Most will not.

The restraint test: if motion is described in a design review, it is too visible. The correct amount of motion is the amount that a viewer would not mention because they are not sure they saw it.

## Section 6 — Colour and Material Palette

The palette is warm paper stock: creams, ambers, ochres, terracottas, and brown inks. It has no blue, no green, no purple, no grey neutrals.

**Warm creams** (`#fffdf8`, `#fffaf0`, `#fff8f3`): page and surface background. The warmest whites available without reading as tinted. They absorb the amber and terracotta tones around them rather than contrasting with them.

**Muted golds** (`#f7e8be`, `#eed9a0`, `#fab642`): accent warmth. Used in editorial rules, ambient fields, and ghost typography at very low opacity. Never used at full opacity in body elements — too bright, too decorative.

**Ember oranges** (`#e34b16`, `#ea5118`, `#efc0af`): brand accent. Reserved for dominant CTAs, the hero art panel, and the editorial anchor rule. The strongest colour in the system. Used sparingly because its warmth creates visual gravity wherever it appears.

**Cocoa browns** (`#7a331b`, `#713522`, `#9a7258`, `#6d5849`): the primary ink palette. Used for headings, body text, metadata, and label elements. The `#713522` heading colour sits at a slightly warmer hue angle than `#7a331b` — this is deliberate, as described in Section 2.3. The description-text browns (`#6d5849`, `#6d5849/70`) are significantly lighter and desaturated — they recede into the background while remaining legible.

**No colour should feel digitally pure.** Digital pure colours — fully saturated, high contrast, spectrally exact — carry the quality of emitted light. Every colour in this palette carries the quality of absorbed warmth. They are the colours of material that has been touched by warmth, not the colours of light that has been mixed on a screen.

## Section 7 — Implementation Rules

These rules are strict. Exceptions require explicit justification against the atmospheric intention documented here.

---

**Never add visible effects without atmospheric justification.**
An effect that a viewer can identify is decoration. Decoration is not atmosphere. Before adding any visual element, confirm that its function is to modulate perception, not to contribute a visible asset.

**Never use perfect grids without compositional interruption.**
A perfect grid announces a system. Introduce micro-offsets, asymmetric column ratios, or spacing cadence variation in every grid layout. The interruption does not need to be large. 2% column asymmetry (`1.02fr / 0.98fr`) is sufficient. The viewer should feel organisation without feeling automation.

**Never use full black text.**
`#000000` or near-black text on warm backgrounds reads as imposed contrast — an element from outside the page's thermal register. Use warm dark browns in the `#6d5849–#7a331b` range.

**Never use hard white surfaces.**
`#ffffff` backgrounds read as digitally unmodulated — no warmth, no material quality. Use warm whites (`#fffdf8`, `#fffaf0`) at reduced opacity (`65–85%`) so that the page warmth reads through them.

**Never increase contrast for "clarity" without considering tone.**
Legibility is important. It is not more important than the thermal register of the composition. If text needs to be more legible, adjust its weight or size before adjusting its colour toward black.

**Never add motion before checking compositional hierarchy.**
Motion directs attention. Adding motion to an element promotes it in the reading order. Before adding any animation, confirm that the animated element belongs at the top of the intended reading sequence.

**Never introduce visual ideas in multiple categories simultaneously.**
One atmospheric layer, one typographic refinement, one spatial adjustment per iteration. Multiple simultaneous changes produce noise rather than signal, and make it impossible to evaluate individual decisions.

**Prefer subtraction over addition.**
When the composition feels dense, remove before adding. When the atmosphere feels flat, check whether existing elements are at appropriate opacity before adding new ones. The system should compound existing assets rather than accumulate new ones.

**When refining, change only one perceptual layer at a time.**
Perceptual layers: typography, spacing, colour, materiality, motion, composition. A single iteration should target one layer. Changing two or more simultaneously makes it impossible to evaluate what produced the improvement or the problem.

## Section 8 — Future Extensions

This design language is a foundation, not a container. It should expand into every surface the product touches — settings, modals, forms, story pages, navigation, onboarding, mobile — with consistent principles but calibrated intensity.

**Settings pages**: calmer than editorial hero moments. Settings are administrative surfaces. They should carry the same warmth and typographic care as the dashboard, but with reduced atmospheric layering — no ambient fields, no warmth pools, no prominent contour backgrounds. The interaction is functional; the environment should not compete for attention.

**Modals and overlays**: treated as focused editorial moments. A modal that appears with the same cinematic curve as the dashboard entrance belongs in this environment. A modal with a generic fade-in does not. Modals should feel like turning to a specific page — a clear shift of focus that maintains thermal register.

**Forms**: the calmest surfaces in the system. Input fields should be warm but not atmospheric. Labels use the eyebrow system. Validation states use warm amber (success) and a desaturated terracotta (error) — never harsh red or green. The form should feel like filling out a physical document, not submitting data to a system.

**Story and editorial pages**: the most expressive surfaces. Typography can be larger and more assertive. White space can be more generous. A story page is where the editorial identity is most fully expressed. The hero treatment of story pages should extend the hero design language of the dashboard: Fraunces at display size, warm photography, atmospheric warmth pooling, the signature letterform motif if appropriate.

**Navigation**: the most restrained surface. Navigation elements should have the lowest visual weight in the composition. They are the architecture of the space — present, functional, not assertive. The wordmark and navigation links should feel typeset, not designed.

**Onboarding**: guided, warm, and unhurried. Onboarding is a user's first experience of the space. It should introduce the atmosphere gradually — lighter on the first screen, progressively warmer as the user moves deeper. The editorial rhythm of the dashboard should be legible even in onboarding, so that arriving at the dashboard feels like a natural continuation, not a new experience.

**Mobile layouts**: the atmospheric system adapts but does not simplify to generic. Mobile surfaces carry warm backgrounds and brown ink text. Type scales appropriately. Ambient fields are smaller and positioned for the narrower canvas. The editorial split of the hero panel collapses gracefully to a stacked composition. The Identity Record moves below the tile grid. The signature letterform motif is hidden at mobile widths — it requires the split layout to work compositionally.

The governing principle for all extensions: **the language must remain consistent, but intensity should adapt by context.** A form that has the same atmospheric density as the editorial hero is overdesigned for its function. A settings page that has none of the warmth of the dashboard feels like a different product. Calibration is the skill. The design language provides the vocabulary; judgment determines the appropriate volume for each surface.
